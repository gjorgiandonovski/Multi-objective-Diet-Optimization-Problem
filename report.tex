\section*{Abstract}

This work addresses the multi-objective weekly diet planning problem from the Bioinspired Algorithms for Optimization (BAO) course. Given a database of 2{,}616 real food items and a user profile with a daily caloric target, the goal is to build a seven-day meal plan covering five daily meals (morning snack, breakfast, lunch, afternoon snack and dinner), where each slot may only be filled by foods from the appropriate category. The problem has two conflicting objectives: minimise the total weekly deviation from the caloric target, and minimise the total weekly deviation from a macronutrient split of 50\% carbohydrates, 27.5\% fat and 22.5\% protein.

We model the problem as a constrained two-objective minimisation over a 77-gene weekly chromosome and solve it with four bioinspired metaheuristics covering both course families: \textbf{NSGA-II} and \textbf{PAES} as evolutionary baselines, and \textbf{MOPSO} and \textbf{P-ACO} as swarm-intelligence methods. Two encodings are compared, direct integer indices and random keys, both behind a common representation interface. A constraint-handling ablation on NSGA-II direct-index pits \emph{repair}, \emph{penalty} and \emph{death-penalty} against each other; we show that, because our domain-aware variator makes infeasibility impossible by construction, the three handlers produce \emph{bit-for-bit identical} quality metrics. From the results section onwards we therefore report NSGA-II direct-index as a single entry and contrast it with the random-key variant, treating the handler comparison as a documented structural equivalence.

The full benchmark comprises 1{,}050 runs across seven configurations (30 replicates $\times$ 5 subjects each), evaluated using hypervolume, IGD, Schott's spacing and Deb's $\Delta$-spread. Statistical analysis follows the BAO chapter~6 protocol: Friedman Aligned Ranks for the omnibus and pairwise Wilcoxon signed-ranks with Holm--Bonferroni correction for the post-hoc.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

\subsection{Context and Motivation}

Diet planning is a common challenge in clinical nutrition, sports science and consumer health. Designing an optimal weekly meal plan requires meeting a personalised daily caloric target $T$ while maintaining a specific macronutrient distribution (50\% carbohydrates, 27.5\% fats, 22.5\% proteins). Balancing these objectives is difficult because improving one can degrade the other --- adding a high-protein item may push the caloric total beyond $T$, while replacing it with a lower-calorie alternative may unbalance the macronutrient ratios.

Constructing feasible plans by hand is impractical given the size of the catalogue and the per-meal category restrictions. From an optimisation standpoint, this is a constrained, combinatorial, multi-objective problem with non-linear macronutrient ratios; exact methods such as Integer Linear Programming are computationally intractable, which makes bioinspired metaheuristics a natural choice because they explore large discrete search spaces efficiently and handle conflicting objectives without gradient information.

\subsection{Formal Problem Definition}
\label{sec:formal_definition}

We formalise the problem through its decision variables, domain constraints and objective functions.

\subsubsection{Decision Variables and Structure}
A candidate solution is a \emph{weekly meal plan} for a single user, spanning 7 days ($d \in \{1, \dots, 7\}$). Each day consists of five distinct meals, partitioned into specific consumption slots:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{Morning \& Afternoon Snacks:} 1 slot each (fruit or sugar items).
    \item \textbf{Breakfast:} 3 slots (1 drink, 2 solid foods).
    \item \textbf{Lunch \& Dinner:} 3 slots each (1 drink, 2 solid foods).
\end{itemize}
This yields 11 slots per day, totalling $77$ slots per weekly plan. A solution is thus represented as a vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{N}^{77}$, where each decision variable $x_j$ ($j \in \{1, \dots, 77\}$) is the index of the food item assigned to slot~$j$.

\subsubsection{Admissible Food Sets and Search Space}
Let $\mathcal{F} = \{1, \dots, 2616\}$ denote the complete catalogue of food indices. Foods are categorised via hierarchical group prefixes (e.g.\ \texttt{FA} for fruits, \texttt{A} for cereals). To ensure culinary and nutritional coherence, each slot $j$ is restricted to a pre-computed \emph{admissible set} $D_j \subseteq \mathcal{F}$, dynamically adjusted based on the user's age:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{Snacks:} Restricted to fruits (\texttt{F}) and sugars (\texttt{S}).
    \item \textbf{Breakfast Drinks:} Dairy (\texttt{BA}, \texttt{BH}), infusions/powders (\texttt{PA}) and juices (\texttt{FE}, \texttt{FC}).
    \item \textbf{Breakfast Foods:} Cereals (\texttt{A}), eggs (\texttt{C}), fruits (\texttt{FA}) and bacon (\texttt{MAA}), explicitly excluding heavy carbs such as rice, pasta or pizza (\texttt{AC}, \texttt{AD}, \texttt{AE}).
    \item \textbf{Lunch/Dinner Drinks:} General beverages (\texttt{P}, \texttt{FC}, \texttt{FE}); alcoholic drinks (\texttt{Q}) are admitted only if age~$\ge 18$.
    \item \textbf{Lunch/Dinner Foods:} Substantive cereals (\texttt{AC, AD, AE, AF}) and any item not classified under breakfast/snack-exclusive categories.
\end{itemize}

A weekly plan $\mathbf{x}$ is \emph{feasible} iff $x_j \in D_j$ for all $j$. The feasible search space has size $\prod_{j=1}^{77} |D_j|$; since $|D_j|$ ranges from dozens to thousands of items, the total number of combinations is astronomically large, reinforcing the need for stochastic search.

\subsubsection{Multi-Objective Optimisation Model}
For a given day $d$, let $K(\mathbf{x},d)$ be the total consumed calories and let $C(\mathbf{x},d)$, $F(\mathbf{x},d)$, $P(\mathbf{x},d)$ be the total grams of carbohydrates, fats and proteins. Using Atwater factors, the caloric contribution from each macronutrient is
\begin{equation}
    K_C^{(d)} = 4\,C(\mathbf{x},d), \quad K_F^{(d)} = 9\,F(\mathbf{x},d), \quad K_P^{(d)} = 4\,P(\mathbf{x},d).
\end{equation}
The total daily macronutrient calories are $K_T^{(d)} = K_C^{(d)} + K_F^{(d)} + K_P^{(d)}$, and the percentage contribution of each macronutrient is $p_{M}^{(d)} = 100 \times K_M^{(d)} / K_T^{(d)}$ for $M \in \{C, F, P\}$. The optimisation problem then reads
\begin{align}
    \text{Minimise} \quad f_1(\mathbf{x}) &= \sum_{d=1}^{7} \bigl| K(\mathbf{x},d) - T \bigr| \label{eq:f1_energy} \\[0.4em]
    \text{Minimise} \quad f_2(\mathbf{x}) &= \sum_{d=1}^{7} \Bigl( \bigl| p_C^{(d)} - 50 \bigr| + \bigl| p_F^{(d)} - 27.5 \bigr| + \bigl| p_P^{(d)} - 22.5 \bigr| \Bigr) \label{eq:f2_macros}
\end{align}
subject to $x_j \in D_j \ \forall j \in \{1, \dots, 77\}$.

\textit{Note on constraints:} the original assignment specified a 55\% carbohydrate target alongside 27.5\% fats and 22.5\% proteins. After consultation with the instructors we corrected this to 50\% so the three percentages sum to 100\%. Consequently, the theoretical optimum for $f_2$ is zero, attained when the daily proportions match the dietary split exactly.

\section{Algorithm Design}

\subsection{Metaheuristics used}

Four metaheuristics span both course families. All four return Pareto archives, share the same feasibility logic, fitness function and metric pipeline, so that any observed difference is attributable to the algorithm itself rather than to evaluation plumbing.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=0.3em]
\item \textbf{NSGA-II} \emph{(evolutionary)} [1]. Selection by non-dominated sorting plus crowding-distance preservation. The de-facto baseline for Pareto-based EAs.
\item \textbf{PAES} \emph{(evolutionary)} [2]. A $(1{+}1)$ evolution strategy paired with an adaptive grid archive. Contrasts NSGA-II both in cardinality (single working solution rather than a population) and in selection mechanism (archive-based rather than dominance-based), which makes it a complementary baseline.
\item \textbf{MOPSO} \emph{(swarm)} [3,5]. Particles move in random-key space, guided by leaders from an external non-dominated archive. The primary leader rule is the Sigma method of Mostaghim and Teich; a random archive pick is available as a fallback. The archive is bounded with crowding-distance pruning.
\item \textbf{P-ACO} \emph{(swarm)}. Ants construct direct-index weekly plans from per-slot pheromone distributions; archive members reinforce the trails without collapsing the objectives into a weighted sum, and crowding-distance pruning preserves diversity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Codifications}
\label{sec:codifications}

Two encodings are implemented under a common \texttt{Representation} interface (\texttt{diet\_bao/representations}). NSGA-II uses both; PAES and P-ACO use direct-index only; MOPSO uses random-key only (required by its velocity update).

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=0.15em]
\item \textbf{Direct-index encoding.} A weekly plan is a length-77 integer vector, one gene per slot. Gene $j$ stores the food ID chosen for that slot, and its admissible domain is the precomputed set $D_j$. The initial generator samples each gene uniformly from $D_j$. Mutation is \emph{domain-aware}: with probability equal to the mutation rate a gene is re-sampled uniformly from $D_j$, otherwise it is left unchanged. Crossover is standard $n$-point crossover; it remains feasible because both parents are feasible and each position can only contain values from its own $D_j$.
\item \textbf{Random-key encoding.} A weekly plan is a length-77 real vector $\mathbf{k} \in [0,1]^{77}$. Decoding maps each $k_j$ to a food in $D_j$ by splitting $[0,1]$ into $|D_j|$ equal-width bins, so \emph{every} vector in $[0,1]^{77}$ decodes to a feasible plan. Mutation uses Gaussian noise ($\sigma=0.1$) with boundary reflection; crossover again uses the default $n$-point operator.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Constraint handling and a structural equivalence result}
\label{sec:constraints}

Three handlers were implemented (\texttt{repair}, \texttt{penalty}, \texttt{death\_penalty}) behind a shared \texttt{ConstraintHandler} protocol. The ablation we report applies them to NSGA-II direct-index, the only configuration in our suite where a violation could in principle occur.

Across 150 replicates per handler we observed that the three handlers produce \emph{bit-for-bit identical} hypervolume, IGD, spacing, $\Delta$-spread, best $f_1$ and best $f_2$. The cause is structural: the domain-aware variator never emits an infeasible candidate. Mutation either leaves a gene unchanged (already valid by construction) or re-samples it directly from $D_j$; $n$-point crossover swaps whole segments between two feasible parents and each position is independently constrained to its own $D_j$. The handler logic is therefore \emph{never invoked}, and all three trajectories coincide.

\emph{Reporting consequence.} The handler comparison is a controlled negative result and we treat it as such. From Section~\ref{sec:results} onwards, NSGA-II is reported as two distinct configurations only --- \texttt{nsga2\_di\_repair} (direct-index) and \texttt{nsga2\_rk\_repair} (random-key); the \texttt{penalty} and \texttt{death} entries are folded into the direct-index row with a separate breakdown for the only quantity that does differ between them, namely runtime overhead from spurious feasibility checks (Table~\ref{tab:handler_runtime}). MOPSO and P-ACO carry \texttt{constraint\_handler: "none"} because their feasible construction mechanisms make any handler redundant.

\subsection{Implementation details}

The project is written in Python 3.10+ and uses \texttt{inspyred} for NSGA-II and PAES. MOPSO and P-ACO are native implementations built on the same fitness and archive interfaces as the EA variants. Supporting dependencies are \texttt{mysql-connector-python} for database access, \texttt{numpy}/\texttt{pandas} for data manipulation, \texttt{scipy.stats} for statistical tests and \texttt{matplotlib} for visualisation. The food database is restored from the course-provided MySQL dump and queried through \texttt{diet\_bao.data}.

Two optional extensions sit alongside the main benchmark: (i) a multiprocessing execution path (\texttt{BenchmarkPlan(n\_jobs>1)}) that distributes the experimental grid across CPU cores without affecting per-run timing; and (ii) a memetic NSGA-II variant that injects a 1-step Lamarckian local search via a \texttt{memetic\_rate} parameter. Both are excluded from the main comparison tables to keep them directly comparable.

The codebase is organised around clear responsibilities:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=0.1em]
\item \texttt{representations/}: \texttt{direct\_index} and \texttt{random\_key} encodings behind a shared protocol.
\item \texttt{constraints/}: \texttt{repair}, \texttt{penalty} and \texttt{death\_penalty} handlers behind a shared protocol.
\item \texttt{metrics/}: pure-Python implementations of 2D hypervolume, IGD, Schott's spacing and Deb's $\Delta$-spread.
\item \texttt{ea/} and \texttt{si/}: algorithm wrappers (\texttt{nsga2\_diet}, \texttt{paes\_diet}, \texttt{mopso\_diet}, \texttt{paco\_diet}).
\item \texttt{experiment/}: benchmark composition (\texttt{AlgorithmConfig}, \texttt{BenchmarkPlan}, \texttt{run\_all\_subjects}) and visualisation helpers.
\item \texttt{stac/}: Wilcoxon signed-ranks, Friedman, Friedman Aligned Ranks, Shaffer's post-hoc and Holm correction.
\end{itemize}
A suite of unit and integration tests is run with \texttt{pytest} before any experiment. Listing~\ref{lst:mut} shows the domain-aware mutation that drives the equivalence result of Section~\ref{sec:constraints}.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=pyEstilo, caption={Domain-aware mutation for direct-index NSGA-II. Because every gene either stays unchanged or is re-sampled from its own $D_j$, the operator cannot emit an infeasible candidate.}, label={lst:mut}]
def variator(random, candidates, args):
    rate = float(args.get("mutation_rate", 0.1))
    out = []
    for cand in candidates:
        mutant = list(cand)
        for i, domain in enumerate(state.per_position):
            if mutant[i] not in domain or random.random() < rate:
                mutant[i] = domain[random.randrange(len(domain))]
        out.append(mutant)
    return out
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Experimental setup}

The seven configurations are executed across the five user profiles with 30 stochastic replicates each, for a total of $7 \times 5 \times 30 = 1{,}050$ runs. Per-replicate seeds are deterministic and unique (\texttt{seed0+r}), making the experiment fully reproducible. The notebook \texttt{main.ipynb} schedules every (subject, configuration, seed) triple through \texttt{concurrent.futures.ProcessPoolExecutor} (\texttt{BenchmarkPlan(n\_jobs=4)} by default) and writes the raw rows to \texttt{experiments/all\_runs.csv}. The parallel execution path only affects wall-clock throughput: each replicate is still timed independently, so the runtime column remains comparable across configurations.

\paragraph{Metrics.} For every run we record the best $f_1$, the best $f_2$, the runtime and the size of the Pareto front. From the raw front we additionally compute four multi-objective indicators:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=0.1em]
\item \textbf{Hypervolume (HV).} 2D area dominated by the front relative to a per-front reference point set adaptively at $1.1\!\times$ the front maxima plus a small offset. Higher is better.
\item \textbf{Inverted generational distance (IGD).} Mean Euclidean distance from each point of a per-subject reference set (the union of all fronts produced by all algorithms on that subject) to the nearest point of the obtained front. Lower is better.
\item \textbf{Schott's spacing.} Standard deviation of consecutive Euclidean distances along the front. Lower is more uniform.
\item \textbf{Delta Spread (Deb's $\Delta$).} The diversity metric taught in BAO chapter~6.2, combining front extent and uniformity: $\Delta = (\sum_j d_j^e + \sum_i |d_i - \bar d|) / (\sum_j d_j^e + |S|\,\bar d)$. Lower is better.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Statistical tests.} Following the BAO chapter~6 protocol (``Comparing Algorithms Output''), our 30 replicates are paired across configurations because they share the same seed schedule, so we use the two-sided \textbf{Wilcoxon signed-ranks test} [9] for 1-vs-1 paired comparisons. The omnibus across configurations uses the \textbf{Friedman Aligned Ranks test} [8], a more powerful variant of plain Friedman that ranks all block-aligned observations together. The N-vs-N post-hoc analysis is pairwise Wilcoxon with \textbf{Holm--Bonferroni correction} [10] (Shaffer's static procedure [11] is computationally equivalent for our $k$ when no tied hypotheses exist).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=0.1em]
\item \textbf{Single dataset.} Only the course-provided NutritionPlanning database is used; findings may not transfer to food catalogues with different category structures.
\item \textbf{Degenerate constraint comparison.} Because the direct-index variator (both mutation and $n$-point crossover) always produces feasible candidates, the three NSGA-II constraint handlers never change the search trajectory and produce identical quality metrics. A meaningful empirical separation would require a variator that can produce infeasible candidates (see Future Work).
\item \textbf{Small tuning sweep.} The hyperparameter grid covers 12--16 cells per algorithm and is evaluated on only two of the five subjects.
\item \textbf{User preferences not used.} The database carries preference, dislike and allergy lists per subject; they are loaded but not yet incorporated into the fitness function or constraints.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Data description}

The MySQL dump provided by the course contains \textbf{2{,}616 food items}, each annotated with a hierarchical food-group prefix and per-item macronutrient content (kcal, carbohydrates, fat and protein, in grams per serving). It also contains \textbf{five user profiles} (ids 1--5) covering ages 17, 30, 40, 55 and 72, with caloric targets ranging from 1{,}401 to 3{,}103 kcal/day. Each profile additionally carries preference, dislike and allergy lists; these are loaded but not used in the present optimisation. All 2{,}616 foods participate in the experiments, and all five subjects are used for the comparison.

\subsection{Discussion}

\paragraph{Random-key NSGA-II is the strongest overall configuration.} Across the full benchmark, \texttt{nsga2\_rk\_repair} leads on hypervolume (1{,}059{,}760), IGD (152.21) and best mean $f_1$ (1{,}105.10) while remaining within 5\% of the fastest NSGA-II variant on runtime (3.62~s vs.\ 3.44~s). The direct-index variant is a close second on HV ($1{,}014{,}630$) and IGD ($197.13$), and the HV gap between the two encodings does not reach statistical significance ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.359$). The likely driver of the random-key advantage is that Gaussian perturbations in $[0,1]^{77}$ explore the decoded categorical space more smoothly than discrete uniform re-sampling, and the equal-width binning lets a single mutation step traverse multiple foods within a slot's admissible set.

\paragraph{The constraint-handler ablation is a controlled negative result.} The three direct-index handlers (\texttt{repair}, \texttt{penalty}, \texttt{death\_penalty}) produce identical hypervolume (1{,}014{,}630), IGD (197.13), spacing (97.71), $\Delta$-spread (0.82), best $f_1$ (1{,}343.37) and best $f_2$ (167.77) across all 150 replicates each, and every pairwise Wilcoxon test between them returns $p_{\mathrm{adj}}=1.0$. The cause is structural rather than statistical: the domain-aware variator cannot produce an infeasible candidate, so the handler branch is unreachable. The only measurable cost difference comes from spurious validity checks: \texttt{repair} runs in 3.44~s on average, while \texttt{penalty} (6.70~s) and \texttt{death\_penalty} (6.66~s) pay a roughly $2\times$ overhead because they still invoke \texttt{count\_violations} on every individual. This is pedagogically valuable: it makes explicit that a feasibility-preserving representation renders explicit constraint handling moot, a property the report's Future Work section turns into a concrete experimental proposal.

\paragraph{PAES is the best per-point macronutrient solver but the weakest Pareto coverer.} \texttt{paes\_di\_repair} records the lowest mean best $f_2$ across all configurations ($123.92$), meaning its archive members are individually closest to the prescribed macronutrient split. However, its hypervolume ($401{,}023$) is roughly $2.5\times$ smaller than NSGA-II's, reflecting the intrinsic trade-off of a $(1{+}1)$-ES: single-trajectory search can exploit one region deeply but cannot match the breadth of a population-based method, even with an enlarged generation budget (800).

\paragraph{Swarm methods diverge sharply.} \texttt{mopso\_rk} is the fastest method in the benchmark (mean runtime 2.56~s) and achieves a mid-range hypervolume ($454{,}137$); its HV is indistinguishable from PAES ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.451$) but significantly higher than P-ACO. \texttt{paco\_di}, by contrast, occupies the worst position on every metric: lowest mean HV ($245{,}278$), high IGD ($549.00$), and a runtime of $337.53$~s --- roughly $100\times$ MOPSO and $80\times$ NSGA-II direct-index. The combination of low quality and high cost is a strong signal that the current pheromone update loop is not implemented efficiently, and we list its profiling and optimisation as the most impactful Future Work item.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

This project tackled the multi-objective weekly diet planning problem with two evolutionary algorithms (NSGA-II, PAES), two swarm-intelligence algorithms (MOPSO, P-ACO), two solution encodings (direct-index and random-key) and three constraint-handling strategies (repair, penalty, death-penalty). The full benchmark covers $1{,}050$ runs across seven configurations (30 replicates $\times$ 5 subjects each), evaluated on hypervolume, IGD, Schott's spacing and Deb's $\Delta$-spread.

The strongest configuration is \texttt{nsga2\_rk\_repair}, with mean hypervolume $1{,}059{,}760$ and IGD $152.21$ at $3.62$~s per run. The direct-index NSGA-II variant is a close second (HV $1{,}014{,}630$, IGD $197.13$, 3.44~s) and the HV gap does not reach statistical significance. PAES delivers the lowest per-plan macronutrient deviation ($f_2 = 123.92$) but the narrowest front (HV $401{,}023$); MOPSO is the fastest method (2.56~s) at mid-range quality; P-ACO trails on every metric and is by far the slowest ($337.53$~s per run).

The constraint-handler ablation produced a clean structural finding: because the direct-index variator cannot emit an infeasible candidate, \emph{repair}, \emph{penalty} and \emph{death-penalty} share identical trajectories and bit-for-bit identical quality metrics, with the only measurable difference being a $\sim$2$\times$ runtime overhead in \emph{penalty}/\emph{death-penalty} from spurious validity checks. This collapse turned what would otherwise be three separate result rows into a single reportable entry plus a runtime side-table --- a much cleaner presentation, and a pedagogically useful demonstration that a feasibility-preserving representation makes explicit constraint handling redundant.

\subsection*{Future work}

The following directions are \emph{not yet implemented} and represent natural next steps.

\paragraph{1. Unrestricted variator for a meaningful constraint comparison.} The most direct extension is to replace the domain-aware mutation with an operator that can emit infeasible candidates (e.g.\ sampling each gene from the full catalogue rather than from $D_j$). This would let repair, penalty and death-penalty diverge in behaviour, turning the present structural equivalence into a genuine empirical comparison.

\paragraph{2. Preference-aware objectives and constraints.} The database provides per-subject preference, dislike and allergy lists that are currently loaded but unused. Incorporating them --- as a soft penalty for disliked foods or a hard exclusion for allergens --- would produce more personalised and practically usable meal plans.

\paragraph{3. Profile and accelerate the P-ACO pheromone loop.} P-ACO achieves the worst hypervolume (245{,}278) at the highest cost (337.53~s per run). The combination strongly suggests an inefficient inner loop in the pheromone update rather than an algorithmic limitation; profiling and a vectorised rewrite would make it competitive on both axes.

\paragraph{4. Denser hyperparameter tuning.} The current sweep covers 12--16 cells per algorithm on two of the five subjects. A denser grid on all five subjects would strengthen the conclusions and reduce sensitivity to the tuning sample.
